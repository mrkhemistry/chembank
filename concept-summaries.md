## Physical Chemistry

### 1. Atomic structure
1. **Subatomic particles & isotopes** — Proton number \(Z\) defines the element; mass number \(=\) protons \(+\) neutrons. Isotopes share \(Z\) but differ in neutrons, so they are chemically identical but differ in mass. Relative atomic mass is the weighted average of isotopic masses (from the mass spectrum).
2. **Electronic configuration & orbitals** — Electrons fill orbitals (s, p, d) in order of increasing energy following Aufbau, Hund's rule and the Pauli principle. Know the 4s/3d anomaly: 4s fills *before* 3d but is removed *first* on ionisation.
3. **Ionisation energy (IE)** — Energy to remove one mole of electrons from one mole of gaseous atoms/ions. Explain trends (across a period, down a group) using nuclear charge, shielding and distance.
4. **Evidence from successive IEs** — Large "jumps" reveal shell structure; small dips (e.g. Group 13 and Group 16) are evidence for sub-shells and electron-pair repulsion.

### 2. Chemical bonding
1. **Ionic vs covalent vs dative** — Ionic = electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions; covalent = shared pair; dative (coordinate) = both electrons from one atom.
2. **Shapes of molecules (VSEPR)** — Electron pairs repel to minimise repulsion; lone-pair repulsion \(>\) bond-pair. Predict shape and bond angles (linear, trigonal planar, tetrahedral, bent, pyramidal, octahedral).
3. **Electronegativity & polarity** — Polar bonds arise from \(\Delta\)EN; whether a *molecule* is polar depends on shape/symmetry (net dipole).
4. **Intermolecular forces** — id–id (dispersion), pd–pd, and hydrogen bonding; these determine physical properties (mp, bp, solubility, viscosity).
5. **Metallic bonding** — Lattice of cations in a sea of delocalised electrons; explains conductivity and malleability.

### 3. Mole
1. **The mole & Avogadro constant** — \(1\ \text{mol} = 6.02\times10^{23}\) particles; \(n = \dfrac{m}{M}\).
2. **Amounts in solution & gases** — \(n = cV\) for solutions; molar gas volume \(= 24\ \text{dm}^3\) at r.t.p.
3. **Stoichiometry** — Use mole ratios from balanced equations; identify the limiting reagent; calculate % yield and % purity.
4. **Empirical & molecular formulae** — Determine from composition or combustion data.
5. **Titration & back-titration calculations** — Relate concentrations and volumes via the mole ratio.

### 4. Gases
1. **Ideal gas equation** — \(pV = nRT\) with consistent SI units; use to find \(M\), \(n\) or a missing variable.
2. **Kinetic theory assumptions** — Negligible molecular volume, no intermolecular forces, elastic collisions, mean KE \(\propto T\).
3. **Real vs ideal deviations** — Gases deviate most at high pressure and low temperature (finite molecular volume and intermolecular attractions matter).

### 5. Thermochemistry
1. **Enthalpy change \(\Delta H\)** — Exothermic (\(-\)) vs endothermic (\(+\)); read enthalpy profile diagrams and activation energy.
2. **Standard enthalpy definitions** — Formation, combustion, neutralisation, atomisation etc., each *per mole* under standard conditions.
3. **Hess's Law** — Enthalpy is a state function; use energy cycles. \(\Delta H_{\text{rxn}} = \sum \Delta H_f(\text{products}) - \sum \Delta H_f(\text{reactants})\).
4. **Bond energy calculations** — \(\Delta H \approx \sum(\text{bonds broken}) - \sum(\text{bonds formed})\); values are averages, so results are approximate.

### 6. Thermodynamics
1. **Lattice energy & Born–Haber cycle** — Define LE and construct the cycle; LE increases with higher ionic charge and smaller ionic radius.
2. **Enthalpy of hydration & solution** — Dissolving = breaking the lattice \(+\) hydrating the ions; the sign of \(\Delta H_{\text{sol}}\) follows from their relative magnitudes.
3. **Entropy \(\Delta S\)** — A measure of disorder; predict its sign from changes in gas moles, state, dissolving, and temperature.
4. **Gibbs free energy** — \(\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S\); a reaction is feasible when \(\Delta G < 0\). Use it to find the temperature at which feasibility changes.

### 7. Kinetics
1. **Collision theory** — Rate depends on the frequency of *effective* collisions (correct orientation and energy \(\geq E_a\)). Factors: concentration, pressure, temperature, surface area, catalyst.
2. **Rate equation & order** — \(\text{rate} = k[A]^m[B]^n\); order and \(k\) (with units) are found experimentally by initial rates or graphs.
3. **Half-life & graphs** — A constant half-life indicates first order; interpret concentration–time and rate–concentration graphs.
4. **Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution** — Explains why temperature and catalysts increase the fraction of molecules with energy \(\geq E_a\).
5. **Mechanism & rate-determining step** — The rate equation reveals the species (and number) in the slow step; catalysts provide an alternative pathway of lower \(E_a\).

### 8. Chemical equilibrium
1. **Dynamic equilibrium** — Forward and backward rates are equal, macroscopic properties are constant, in a closed system.
2. **\(K_c\) and \(K_p\)** — Write the equilibrium expression; \(K\) is changed *only* by temperature; its magnitude indicates the position of equilibrium.
3. **Le Chatelier's principle** — Predict the effect of concentration, pressure and temperature on position; a catalyst changes neither position nor \(K\).
4. **Equilibrium calculations** — Use ICE tables with concentrations, mole fractions or partial pressures.

### 9. Acid base equilibrium
1. **Brønsted–Lowry & conjugate pairs** — Acid = proton donor, base = proton acceptor; strong vs weak = extent of dissociation.
2. **pH, \(K_a\), \(K_b\), \(K_w\)** — \(\text{pH} = -\log[H^+]\); \(K_w = [H^+][OH^-] = 10^{-14}\) at 298 K; calculate pH of strong and weak acids/bases.
3. **Buffers** — Resist pH change via a conjugate acid–base pair; \(\text{pH} = pK_a + \log\dfrac{[\text{salt}]}{[\text{acid}]}\).
4. **Titration curves & indicators** — Recognise curve shapes for strong/weak combinations; choose an indicator whose \(pK_{\text{in}}\) lies in the equivalence-point jump.
5. **Salt hydrolysis** — Salts of weak acids/bases give non-neutral solutions.

### 10. Solubility
1. **Solubility product \(K_{sp}\)** — Write the expression (with correct units) for a sparingly soluble salt.
2. **\(K_{sp}\) ↔ solubility** — Convert between them for a given salt.
3. **Common ion effect** — Solubility decreases when a common ion is already present.
4. **Predicting precipitation** — A precipitate forms when the ionic product \(>\) \(K_{sp}\).

### 11. Redox
1. **Oxidation numbers** — Assign using the rules; identify what is oxidised/reduced and the oxidising/reducing agents.
2. **Half-equations & balancing** — Combine half-equations and balance redox equations in acidic or alkaline media.
3. **Redox titrations** — Perform calculations (e.g. \(\text{MnO}_4^-\), \(\text{I}_2\)/thiosulfate) using the mole ratio.
4. **Disproportionation** — One species is simultaneously oxidised and reduced.

### 12. Electrolysis
1. **Discharge at electrodes** — Cations reduced at the cathode, anions oxidised at the anode; predict products for molten vs aqueous by selective discharge (\(E^\circ\), concentration, electrode material).
2. **Quantitative electrolysis** — \(Q = It\) and \(n(e^-) = \dfrac{Q}{F}\); relate charge to the amount deposited or liberated.
3. **Applications** — Electroplating, purification of metals, and extraction.

### 13. Electrochemical cell
1. **Standard electrode potential \(E^\circ\)** — Measured against the standard hydrogen electrode (0 V); understand the sign convention.
2. **Cell EMF** — \(E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = E^\circ_{\text{cathode}} - E^\circ_{\text{anode}}\); a more positive \(E^\circ\) means a stronger oxidising agent / more readily reduced.
3. **Feasibility** — A positive \(E^\circ_{\text{cell}}\) means the reaction is feasible; link to \(\Delta G = -nFE^\circ_{\text{cell}}\). Note limitations (kinetics, non-standard conditions).
4. **Cell construction & notation** — Draw and interpret cell diagrams; distinguish galvanic from electrolytic cells.

## Inorganic Chemistry

### 14. Periodic table
1. **Periodicity** — Properties repeat across periods because of recurring electronic structure.
2. **Trends across Period 3** — Atomic radius decreases, IE and electronegativity increase; melting point depends on structure (giant metallic → giant covalent Si peak → simple molecular).
3. **Oxides & chlorides of Period 3** — Bonding shifts ionic → covalent; oxide character basic → amphoteric → acidic; predict the pH when they react with water.
4. **Explaining the trends** — Use nuclear charge, shielding, and structure & bonding.

### 15. Group 2
1. **Trends down the group** — Atomic radius increases, IE decreases, reactivity (metallic character) increases.
2. **Reactions with water and oxygen** — Form hydroxides/oxides; vigour increases down the group.
3. **Solubility trends** — Sulfate solubility *decreases* down the group; hydroxide solubility *increases* down the group.
4. **Thermal stability of carbonates & nitrates** — Increases down the group as cation polarising power falls; know the decomposition products.

### 16. Group 17
1. **Physical trends** — Down the group, id–id forces and boiling points increase, volatility decreases, and colour deepens.
2. **Oxidising power decreases down the group** — Shown by halogen–halide displacement reactions.
3. **Reducing power of halides increases down the group** — Seen in reactions with concentrated \(\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4\) (products range from HCl to \(\text{H}_2\text{S}\)).
4. **Reactions of halide ions** — With \(\text{AgNO}_3\) (precipitate colours and solubility in \(\text{NH}_3\)) for identification.
5. **Disproportionation of chlorine** — In water and in cold/hot alkali.

### 17. Transition elements
1. **Definition & configuration** — d-block elements forming at least one stable ion with a partially filled d sub-shell.
2. **Variable oxidation states** — Arise from the similar energies of the 4s and 3d sub-shells.
3. **Complex ions & ligands** — Dative bonding from ligands; know coordination number, shape, ligand exchange and stability constants.
4. **Colour** — d–d transitions: ligands split the d-orbitals, and absorption of visible light gives the complementary colour.
5. **Catalytic behaviour** — Homogeneous (variable oxidation states) and heterogeneous (surface adsorption) catalysis.

## Organic Chemistry

### 18. Introduction to Organic
1. **Nomenclature & functional groups** — IUPAC naming, homologous series, and general formulae.
2. **Isomerism** — Structural (chain, positional, functional group) and stereoisomerism (cis–trans/E–Z and optical/enantiomers); recognise chirality.
3. **Bond breaking** — Homolytic fission → radicals; heterolytic fission → ions. Use curly arrows; identify nucleophiles and electrophiles.
4. **Reaction types** — Overview of addition, substitution, elimination, condensation, oxidation and reduction.

### 19. Alkane
1. **Structure & lack of reactivity** — Saturated, non-polar C–C and C–H bonds make alkanes generally unreactive.
2. **Free-radical substitution** — With halogens under UV: initiation, propagation, termination; limitation is a mixture of products.
3. **Combustion** — Complete vs incomplete; use as fuels and the associated environmental issues (CO, \(\text{CO}_2\), \(\text{NO}_x\)).

### 20. Alkene
1. **The C=C bond** — \(\sigma + \pi\), sp\(^2\), planar, with restricted rotation (→ cis–trans isomerism); a region of high electron density.
2. **Electrophilic addition** — Mechanism with \(\text{X}_2\), HX and \(\text{H}_2\text{O}\)/\(\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4\); Markovnikov's rule explained by carbocation stability.
3. **Oxidation** — Cold dilute \(\text{KMnO}_4\) → diol; hot \(\text{KMnO}_4\) → cleavage (carboxylic acid/ketone/\(\text{CO}_2\)).
4. **Addition polymerisation & reduction** — Forms poly(alkenes); hydrogenation gives alkanes.

### 21. Arene
1. **Structure & stability** — A delocalised \(\pi\) system; evidence includes enthalpy of hydrogenation and equal C–C bond lengths.
2. **Electrophilic substitution (not addition)** — Preserves aromaticity: nitration, halogenation, Friedel–Crafts alkylation/acylation.
3. **Directing & activating effects** — Substituents are o,p-directing/activating or m-directing/deactivating; compare benzene's reactivity with alkenes.
4. **Ring vs side-chain reactions** — e.g. oxidation of an alkyl side chain to –COOH.

### 22. Halogenoalkane
1. **Nucleophilic substitution** — \(S_N1\) vs \(S_N2\) (mechanism, kinetics, effect of 1°/2°/3° structure); nucleophiles include \(\text{OH}^-\), \(\text{CN}^-\), \(\text{NH}_3\).
2. **Elimination** — With hot ethanolic KOH to form alkenes; know substitution vs elimination conditions.
3. **Reactivity trend** — C–I is the weakest C–X bond, so iodoalkanes hydrolyse fastest (shown with \(\text{AgNO}_3\)).
4. **Uses & environmental impact** — e.g. CFCs and ozone depletion.

### 23. Hydroxyl Compounds
1. **Classification & properties** — 1°/2°/3°; hydrogen bonding gives high boiling points and water solubility.
2. **Oxidation** — 1° → aldehyde → carboxylic acid; 2° → ketone; 3° resistant (\(\text{KMnO}_4\)/\(\text{K}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7\)).
3. **Substitution & dehydration** — With Na (→ \(\text{H}_2\)), esterification, conversion to halogenoalkane (HX/\(\text{PX}\)/\(\text{SOCl}_2\)), dehydration → alkene.
4. **Tri-iodomethane (iodoform) test** — Positive for the \(\text{CH}_3\text{CH(OH)}\)– group.

### 24. Carbonyl Compound
1. **C=O polarity** — sp\(^2\) carbonyl carbon is electrophilic.
2. **Nucleophilic addition** — With HCN (→ hydroxynitrile) and reduction with \(\text{NaBH}_4\)/\(\text{LiAlH}_4\) → alcohol.
3. **Distinguishing aldehydes from ketones** — Aldehydes are oxidised by Tollens' (silver mirror) and Fehling's; ketones are not.
4. **Identification tests** — 2,4-DNPH detects a carbonyl; the tri-iodomethane test is positive for \(\text{CH}_3\text{CO}\)– (methyl ketones).

### 25. Carboxylic Acid
1. **Acidity** — Weakly acidic; the carboxylate is resonance-stabilised. Electron-withdrawing groups (e.g. Cl) increase acidity; reactions with metals/carbonates/bases distinguish acids from alcohols and phenols.
2. **Esterification & acyl chlorides** — Acid + alcohol \(\rightleftharpoons\) ester + \(\text{H}_2\text{O}\); acyl chlorides are far more reactive; ester hydrolysis (acidic/alkaline saponification).
3. **Reduction** — \(\text{LiAlH}_4\) reduces –COOH to a primary alcohol.
4. **Derivatives** — Esters, acyl chlorides and amides: relative reactivity and interconversion.

### 26. Nitrogen Compound
1. **Amines as bases** — The N lone pair accepts a proton; compare basicity of aliphatic amines, ammonia and aromatic amines (alkyl vs aryl effects).
2. **Preparation of amines** — Reduction of nitro compounds/nitriles, or from a halogenoalkane + \(\text{NH}_3\).
3. **Amides & amino acids** — The amide/peptide bond; amino acids exist as zwitterions with an isoelectric point and show acid–base behaviour.
4. **Amines as nucleophiles** — React with acyl chlorides and halogenoalkanes.

### 27. Polymers
1. **Addition polymerisation** — From unsaturated monomers (e.g. alkenes); identify the repeat unit and monomer.
2. **Condensation polymerisation** — Polyesters and polyamides (nylon, Terylene, peptides) with loss of a small molecule (\(\text{H}_2\text{O}\)/HCl); deduce monomers from the polymer and vice versa.
3. **Hydrolysis** — Condensation polymers can be hydrolysed (addition polymers cannot) — relate to biodegradability.
4. **Properties, uses & disposal** — Including recycling and the environmental impact of non-biodegradable plastics.
